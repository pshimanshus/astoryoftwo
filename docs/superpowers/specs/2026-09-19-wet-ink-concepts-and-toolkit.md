# Wet Ink — concept panel + toolkit (run wf_0abb22e4-1dd)

Three concepts were designed independently, each required to state its own strongest counter-argument.

## Wet Ink

**Thesis.** The app is not nine screens. It is one sheet of paper, never cut, never cross-faded, that you unfold, choose on, speak into, watch print, turn over, stamp, tear off, fold and seal. Every step is a state of that sheet. The single technical idea holding it together is one uniform — `uGrainDir`, the paper's machine direction at 12° — which governs both how ink wicks (1.8x further along grain than across it, which is why real ink makes a lozenge and not a circle) and which way the sheet buckles when it gets wet (ridges run parallel to grain, because a constrained sheet that swells cross-grain has nowhere to go but up). That shared uniform is why the whole thing reads as one material instead of a stack of effects. This is brand-true because @a.storyof.two's entire promise is that something handmade and physical is being made for you out of what you said; it is innovative because the thing nobody simulates is that paper *responds* — wet paper cockles, pressed paper stays pressed, torn paper leaves fibre whiskers, burnished creases reflect differently. Ambition here is spent on physical fidelity, not on effects: there is no bloom, no chromatic aberration, no particle, no glow. The "how did they do that" has to come from the paper being alive.

**Signature moment.** On the record screen, while you talk, ink wicks out from a nib at the sheet's centre in a lozenge — measurably longer along the paper's grain than across it — and the sheet visibly buckles where it is wet, low ridges running parallel to the grain at roughly 9mm wavelength, caught by a 14° raking light. The Caveat seconds counter sits on that buckled paper: its baseline tilts as a ridge passes under it. Where you paused and the ink pooled, it is genuinely darker — Beer-Lambert `paper * exp(-k·a)`, not more opacity. When you stop, the sheet dries over 1.8s on a diffusion-limited curve (fast, then very slow) and relaxes — but not all the way. About 18% of the buckle is permanent, because paper that got wet stays buckled. It is still there four screens later, raking across your finished carousel. That residual is the whole concept in one number.

**Self-declared weakness.** The concept spends its entire innovation budget on a material the user is not looking at. On record, their eyes are on the mic button and the seconds counter. On printing, on the copy. On reveal, on their own carousel. The cockle, the 1.8x wick anisotropy, the fibre whiskers on the tear — these live in peripheral vision, which is precisely where the visual system discards high-frequency detail. There is a real chance that after four weeks, a user's entire summary is "the paper looked nice" — which a good static texture and framer-motion would have bought in three days for zero bundle. And the physics I am proudest of is the physics that is least legible at this size: a 1.8 ratio turns a circle into a lozenge, and at thumb-scale on a 390px frame almost nobody can tell those apart. The defence — that fidelity accumulates into an unconscious sense that the thing is real — is something I believe and cannot measure, and unmeasurable value is exactly how design budgets get burned.

The falsifiable test, which I would run before committing week two: build ONLY the record screen's wet-cockle and the printing→reveal page turn. Ship to twenty people. Watch whether anyone films their screen or sends it to someone. If nobody does, the remaining three weeks are decoration and the right move is to keep those two moments, bake everything else, and stop.

The second weakness is structural and I want it on the record rather than buried: the "one sheet" conceit fights the actual flow in two places. The photos screen is an interaction with twelve objects and the reveal screen is an interaction with a stack of cards — neither is naturally one sheet. I bent them into a contact sheet and a card pile, and both are defensible, but those are the two screens where the metaphor is doing work rather than being obviously true. If a reviewer says the contact-sheet framing feels imposed, they are seeing something real, and the honest fix is to let those two screens be sheets-plural rather than to force the conceit and have it read as a gimmick.

Third, smaller: the permanent-impression rules (embossing that never lifts, ink that dries and stays, cockle residual) are good design and bad for anyone who explores by tapping around. A user who taps all five stars to see what happens has permanently marked their letter. I think that is correct and I would ship it, but it is a deliberate refusal to be forgiving, and it will generate at least one piece of user feedback that reads as a bug report.

## The Tunnel Book

**Thesis.** A tunnel book — the 18th-century peepshow book — is literally a stack of illustrated paper cut-outs held apart in depth, viewed through a window in the front cover, lit by a slot above. It is the only historical object that is simultaneously (a) hand-drawn paper, ink and intimacy, and (b) the exact technical shape Angle B demands: flat illustrated layers separated in Z, real perspective, genuine occlusion, a single motivated light, dust in the air between the sheets. Tunnel books were made as love tokens. So: the phone is not a screen showing a diorama, it is the front cover of one, and the nine steps in src/flow/steps.ts are nine camera stations inside one continuous paper room that the user travels down. This resolves the brief's tension without compromise — every 3D move is a real dolly with real occlusion and real rack focus, and none of it is anonymous, because the depth is built from Aachu & Zuv's own cut-out figures and the user's own photographs flying back into the space to become its walls. It also turns the hard drei constraint into the concept: drei's single View.Port means the WebGL layer sits entirely behind the DOM UI, which is precisely the tunnel book's front cover — the Caveat type, the MicButton and the HandDrawnArrow are the paper you look through, and the space is behind them. The room is built from four things the brand already owns: the couple's line, their handwriting, the user's photographs, and one warm light.

**Signature moment.** THE TURN — 1.1s at the printing→reveal seam, and it is the only move in the app over one second. Setup: for the whole 15s of PrintingScreen the two illustrated cut-outs of Aachu & Zuv stand at z=-2.6 with their BACKS to the camera, while the user's chosen photographs fly away from the prop layer, flatten, desaturate toward ink and tile themselves into the back wall behind them — so by second 15 the couple is standing in a room built out of the user's own pictures. Then reveal fires. Frame by frame at 60fps: 0–260ms, camera dollies z=+0.2 → -0.9 and the torn foreground paper edge at z=-0.35 swells, goes to 9px of blur and whips past the lens out of frame (the camera has passed THROUGH a plane — the single strongest depth cue available and impossible in CSS); 260–700ms, both cut-outs rotate 180° on Y, Aachu leading Zuv by 90ms, each sheet bowing 0.28 units through the turn so it flexes like paper rather than pivoting like a card, their painted cut-out shadows rotating and shortening with them across the back wall; at t≈550ms each figure passes exactly edge-on and vanishes to a one-pixel ink line for about three frames — the instant where the viewer sees, unmistakably, that the people they have been looking at are sheets of paper standing in a lit room; 700–1100ms they come round facing camera, now drawn in the finished style, while uFocalZ racks from the back wall to -2.6 so they sharpen out of a warm blur and the photo-wall behind them goes soft. It ends on two illustrated people looking at you, in a paper room made of your own photographs, with the light still on. That is the screenshot; the three edge-on frames are what gets filmed and slowed down.

**Self-declared weakness.** Three arguments, in descending strength.

First and worst: the entire identity of this theme rests on two illustrated cut-outs that do not exist yet, and no shader can substitute for them. Strip the figure layer and what remains — depth-separated planes, rack focus, dust in a light shaft — is a stock depth-map diorama demo, the precise failure the brief names. So the concept carries a hard external dependency on illustration production, and worse, the WebGL work cannot be honestly evaluated until that art lands. A team that builds the engine first will spend two weeks staring at grey placeholder rectangles in a lit room, unable to tell whether the idea is beautiful or embarrassing, and every intermediate review will be a vibes argument. The de-risk is to commission the two figures and the authored still in week one and build Phase 2 against the real art, but that inverts the usual order and costs schedule.

Second: the metaphor fights the flow, and I have papered over that with variety rather than solving it. Five of the nine screens — hello, photos, rate, and substantially record and send — are forms. Text inputs, a scrolling three-column grid, five tap targets. A form does not want to be in a room; it wants to be flat, legible and still. So on those screens the memory space is reduced to a blurred backdrop plus one small reactive flourish (a handwriting watermark, a falling twin, an ink star), which means I am paying for a WebGL renderer, a 5 MB texture budget, a 165 KB async chunk and a genuine mid-range fill-rate risk for a payoff that is fully realised on exactly three screens: review, printing, reveal. A cheaper competing theme that spent everything on printing→reveal and left the other six screens as paper-and-ink DOM would deliver most of the felt experience for perhaps 30% of the cost and none of the phone risk. My honest defence is that the three flourishes are individually good and cumulatively make the space feel continuous rather than switched on for a set piece — but that is an argument about coherence, and coherence is a weaker claim than impact.

Third, specific and awkward: "screens are places you travel between" has a failure mode on a phone that gets worse exactly where users hesitate. If the camera moves on a step change, it must reverse on back, and FlowProvider has prevStep, so back is real. A user oscillating between hello and photos while they settle on names and pictures gets a room that sways. I mitigated with two zero-move stations, a 0.8× reverse duration and moves capped at 0.5 units — but the mitigation is an admission that the central metaphor is least comfortable in exactly the part of the flow where people take the longest.

## The Second Drum

**Thesis.** The app is already a machine metaphor it never cashes: a PhotoSelectScreen that is really a copyboard, a RecordScreen that is really an ink intake, a PrintingScreen that is really a press run, a SendScreen that is really an envelope. Angle C builds the machine those screens are already describing — one small tabletop two-colour risograph on a dark wooden desk, lit by a single warm lamp, and makes the nine steps in src/flow/steps.ts nine stations along its paper path. The camera never cuts; advancing a step is a dolly along the bed, which is why this is a system and not one great PrintingScreen. The brand argument is the machine's single constraint: it prints in exactly two colours, --ink and --accent, one drum each. Two drums, two names in the composing stick, two mugs on the desk. The drums never quite register, and where the two passes overlap, Beer–Lambert multiply produces a third tone (#241b19) that neither drum can print alone. That is the whole brand stated as mechanism rather than as decoration, and it justifies literal 3D: you cannot composite two screened halftone passes at two screen angles through a physically deforming sheet in CSS. Warmth comes from scale and tempo, not from softness — the press is 40cm of scuffed wood and brass, everything runs at hand-crank speed (one drum revolution per 2.2s), there is handwriting on masking tape stuck to the drum, and the second mug moves beside the first or across the desk into the dark depending on the answer given on HelloScreen.

**Signature moment.** At 13200ms into PrintingScreen's 15-second run, the sheet already looks finished. Drum one has laid --ink through the stencil at a 105° screen angle, the halftone has resolved, the image is complete and correct in one colour — and the machine stops. Then the second drum indexes in with two soft clacks, the sheet re-feeds, and drum two lays --accent over it at 45° with 1.2px of deliberate misregistration. The camera pushes to macro on a single overlap region and holds for 400ms: two dot grids at different angles, neither aligned, and in the overlap a dark warm tone (--overprint #241b19) that is not on the machine's palette because it only exists where both drums touched the same fibre. Then it pulls back. That 400ms frame is the screenshot: two things, imperfectly aligned, making a colour neither could make alone. It is also the only moment in the app where the camera goes macro on the print itself, so it is unmistakable and unrepeatable.

**Self-declared weakness.** Two, and the second is the dangerous one.

The practical objection: this concept has an asset dependency the repo does not have and cannot shortcut. Every other direction degrades toward "nice paper" if the execution is only 70% there. This one degrades toward "a mediocre videogame prop on a phone", because a wrong shader is abstract and forgivable while a wrong *object* is recognisably wrong — everyone knows what a small machine on a desk should look like, and a grey under-modelled press with a flat brass crank reads instantly as a Blender starter file. That is precisely the anonymity failure the brief forbids, arrived at from the opposite direction. It is worse than it sounds because the WebP poster on LandingScreen is a render of the same model, so a weak machine is the very first thing anyone sees, before a single line of shader work has a chance to save it. Getting this right is art-direction labour with no engineering substitute, and I would not start Phase 1 until a single still of the press at rest is good enough to want as a wallpaper.

The real objection: the machine is a lie about what the app does. There is no press. PrintingScreen's fifteen seconds is `PHASE_TIMINGS` and a `setTimeout` in src/services/fakeGeneration.ts, and the beats it "prints" come from a two-branch template in `deriveStoryBeats`. Building fifteen seconds of loving, registered, two-pass mechanical theatre over that is skeuomorphic fiction — and the more convincingly mechanical it is, the more insistently it asks the user to believe in a process that is not happening. This brand's voice is unusually honest for a product of this kind: "no downloads — this is meant to be given, not saved", "no pressure — follow only if this already feels like you", "Max 3 per person". A beautiful machine would be the one place this app starts performing, and a user who works out that the press is decorative may retroactively distrust the copy too, which is the app's most valuable asset. The partial defences — that the ink volume genuinely comes from their voice via a real AnalyserNode, that the lever genuinely changes the paper path, that the rating is genuinely stamped on the sheet — make the metaphor load-bearing at three points rather than zero, and they are the reason I still believe in the concept. But they do not make the press real. If the team is not prepared to eventually put something real behind it, Angle C is the wrong angle for this brand and the honest move is a quieter direction that never claims a process it does not have.

## Hand-build techniques

- Page-bend vertex shader as the printing→reveal transition — one uProgress uniform bending a subdivided plane, uBend bowing it out of plane mid-turn, front face = the app UI, back face = the finished illustration. Patch into MeshStandardMaterial (via three-custom-shader-material) so the bending sheet still receives the soft contact shadow; that shadow is what sells paper over a textured quad. Catch a click with an invisible backdrop plane — a GPU-side vertex bend is invisible to the CPU raycaster. Blueprint: https://github.com/shiarauzo/book-flip (read src/bendMaterial.ts and src/Book.tsx; 3 stars, no license — reimplement, do not depend).
- Depth-map 2.5D parallax on RevealScreen — one flat illustration plus a hand-painted 3-4 band greyscale depth map displacing UVs by under ~2% driven by slow device tilt. This is literally "depth built from flat illustrated layers" and it costs two textures and one fragment shader. Push the displacement further and it becomes the 2018 fake-3D-photo Instagram trick, which is the opposite of intimate. https://tympanus.net/codrops/2019/02/20/how-to-create-a-fake-3d-image-effect-with-webgl/
- Ink diffusion with Beer–Lambert absorption over a paper substrate — paper * exp(−k * absorbance) so overlapping inks mix like pigment instead of blurring like a Gaussian. Run the ping-ponged fluid solve ONLY for the ~1.5s of record→printing, at 256px velocity / 512px ink, then freeze the result to a texture. Feature-detect float render targets and fall back to a pre-rendered ink-bloom sprite sequence. Solver + paper-mode compositing: https://github.com/stony-su/ink (0 stars, no license — read app.js, take the solver and the compositing, drop its bloom/CA/vignette stack entirely).
- Riso separation, per-ink screening and overprint as the PrintingScreen narrative — lay the ink plates down one at a time as the printing beat. This is a motion idea nobody has templated because it comes from the process, not from a shader gallery. Cap at two or three plates (ink + accent) at 390x844; four-plate CMYK is out of budget on a mid-range phone. https://github.com/chuwd19/overprint (MIT). The zero-install fallback is HalftonePass, already inside three: three/examples/jsm/postprocessing/HalftonePass.js — two channels, small dot radius, real riso screen angles (45°/105°); big dots read as comic-book pop art.
- Ordered (Bayer) dithering as the last pass over the WebGL layer only — the cheapest way to make GPU output read as PRINT rather than as screen, quantising toward the --ink/--paper pair with --accent as the only third tone. gridSize 2-4, grayscaleOnly off; larger grids tip into Game Boy. Full GLSL and the Effect subclass: https://tympanus.net/codrops/2025/06/04/building-a-real-time-dithering-shader/ (companion repo niccolofanton/dithering-shader, MIT). Write it as one hand-rolled full-screen pass rather than installing @react-three/postprocessing for it.
- drei <View> + <View.Port> as the architecture — one canvas scissored into the two or three tracking elements that earn 3D, everything else stays DOM. <Canvas eventSource={container}> re-connects events; frames={1} on views whose tracking element never moves (true inside a fixed PhoneFrame) skips per-frame getBoundingClientRect. This app is static compositions punctuated by transitions — the single best-fitting frameloop mode in the library. The GPU wakes for the transition and the page bend, then sleeps. That, not shader micro-optimisation, is what keeps the phone cool. https://r3f.docs.pmnd.rs/advanced/scaling-performance
- layoutId shared-element travel replacing the flat crossfade — photos → review → printing → reveal is one physical object being handled, and right now it dissolves and reappears nine times identically. Give the card layoutId="story-card", wrap in LayoutGroup, switch mode="wait" to mode="popLayout" (mode="wait" forbids shared elements by definition). layout="position" on text so Caveat and Georgia glyphs never scale-distort; border-radius auto-corrects only when set via the style prop, which this app already does everywhere. Zero new bytes. https://motion.dev/docs/react-layout-animations
- A real motion token scale in src/styles/motion.ts, since tokens.css has exactly one motion token (--ease-soft) that App.tsx doesn't even use: paper-settle (spring, visualDuration 0.5, bounce 0.12 — paper has mass and nearly no bounce), ink-bleed (tween 0.25 easeOut), card-travel (spring, visualDuration 0.45, bounce 0.08). - "ONE persistent canvas outside AnimatePresence or Safari exhausts WebGL contexts" — and App.tsx's AnimatePresence mode="wait" unmounts a screen on every one of the nine steps in src/flow/steps.ts.
