# A Story of Two Visual Direction Bible

The visual planning guide. The image is not decoration for the line — the image
is the proof.

> **Examples are calibration anchors, not reusable templates.** Do not copy
> example setups blindly. Use examples to understand the taste bar, then create a
> fresh scene for the current run.

# Research-Backed Visual Rule

Visual setups must be compared against actual winning visual mechanics in
`.agents/skills/astory/references/source_backed_winner_patterns.md` (the
behavioral-receipt spine, the image-proves-the-line rule, the no-mood-portrait
rule).

For each selected visual setup, the run must record:
- which source-backed visual mechanic it uses (cite the pattern + shortcode),
- why it is fresh for Aachu/Zuv,
- what physical object/gesture/body position carries the emotion,
- what the viewer notices first,
- what the viewer notices second,
- why it is not merely decorating the copy.

If the visual is only generic scene dressing → `VISUAL_ONLY_MOOD_WORDS`.
If it reuses a research example as a template without transformation →
`TEMPLATE_COPYING`.

## Visual philosophy

A Story of Two visuals should feel like a small private moment accidentally made
visible.

**The single hero image is the default format.** A carousel must earn its
existence by adding a new emotional beat per frame; if it only explains what one
image could show, reject it (`CAROUSEL_UNEARNED`).

## Always decide before prompting

- What is the behavioral proof?
- What object, gesture, posture, or distance shows it?
- Where is the emotional reveal located?
- What is the viewer supposed to notice second?
- Are the faces identity-safe?
- Are the hands and limbs simple enough to render cleanly?

## Freshness dimensions

Every post must intentionally vary: setting · camera angle · body blocking · pose
· wardrobe · props · emotional proof · composition · negative space · text
placement.

## Settings beyond couch / chai / balcony (use only when emotionally justified)

small restaurant table · food court corner · late-night dhaba · car parked
outside a takeaway place · grocery aisle · train platform bench · airport waiting
area · roadside momo stall · wedding buffet corner · hotel breakfast table ·
cinema snack counter · rainy street food stall · kitchen counter after a long day
· terrace with delivery bags · hallway outside apartment door · quiet café window
seat.

## Composition principles

- One clear emotional action per image.
- One secondary reveal for rewatch value.
- Keep faces readable when identity matters.
- Keep hands simple if the scene already involves food, phones, bags, or props.
- Avoid crowded background humans unless essential.
- Avoid tiny wide shots when couple identity matters.
- Prefer mid-shot or medium-wide for recurring-character continuity.

## Food-stealing visual lesson

- Weak: Aachu steals from Zuv's plate; Zuv looks annoyed. (The meme everyone
  already knows.)
- Stronger: Zuv mock-guards his plate while an untouched second plate waits near
  Aachu. (The plate reveals he knew her real answer before she admitted it.)
- Strongest: the viewer notices the second plate *after* noticing the stealing —
  that delayed recognition creates the send/share moment.

## Visual proof examples — love as anticipation

- second plate already ordered
- sweater already kept beside her
- charger already plugged in near her side
- umbrella already angled over her
- seat already saved
- spicy food already separated because he knows she'll complain
- extra napkins already near her
- playlist already changed before she asks

## Anatomy risk policy

Every visual plan must simplify: visible hands · crossed arms · forks/spoons ·
overlapping bodies · background people · fingers near food · hands near faces ·
hugging from behind · tangled limbs.

For food scenes:
- keep only 2–3 visible hands if possible
- avoid multiple hands crossing over plates
- avoid fork/finger close-ups
- avoid tiny distorted hands
- use simple silhouettes and clear arm origins
