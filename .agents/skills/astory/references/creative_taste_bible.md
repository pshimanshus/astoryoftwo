# A Story of Two Creative Taste Bible

The highest-level taste guide for `@a.storyof.two`. Read this before any concept,
copy, or visual work. It outranks cleverness and completeness.

> **Examples are calibration anchors, not reusable templates.** Do not copy
> example lines blindly. Use examples to understand the taste bar, then create a
> fresh line and scene for the current run.

# Research-Backed Taste Rule

The taste benchmark must be grounded in real A Story of Two V2 research,
winner-bank evidence, carousel exports, engagement evals, or brain lessons.

Invented examples are allowed only as private reasoning aids. They are not
canonical benchmark evidence.

Before approving a creative package, compare it against **source-backed winner
patterns** from `.agents/skills/astory/references/source_backed_winner_patterns.md`
and `.agents/skills/astory/references/taste_benchmark.md`. The goal is not to copy
winners — it is to preserve the winning mechanism while creating a fresh Aachu/Zuv
moment.

**Actual winning evidence outranks generic taste examples.**

## What the page is

A Story of Two captures small couple behaviors that reveal love without announcing
love.

The post should feel like:
> "I have seen this exact thing."
not:
> "This is a relationship quote."

## The working order (binding)

**Find the behavior. Let the image prove it. Let the copy barely touch it.**
**Do not start with copy** — start with the behavior, then the visual proof, then
the format, and only then the words.

Core principle: **same people, same world, new moment.**

## Taste ladder

### Level 1 — Fail
Generic quote, meme, or obvious relationship trope.
- Example: "Couples who share food together stay together."
- Why it fails: no specific observation; anyone could write it.

### Level 2 — Weak
Recognizable but too staged or explained.
- Example: "She says she doesn't want food but steals mine."
- Why it fails: the observation is real, but the execution is plain meme logic.

### Level 3 — Good
Specific behavior with visual proof.
- Example: "She said no. He ordered anyway."
- Why it works: the love is in what he anticipated, not in what they said.

### Level 4 — Strong
The image reveals the emotional truth almost without copy.
- Example visual: he guards his plate dramatically, but an untouched second plate
  is already waiting near her.
- Why it works: the joke, intimacy, and softness are all visible.

### Level 5 — A Story of Two
A tiny behavior becomes emotional proof.
- Not "she steals food."
- The real truth is: "He plans for the answer she won't admit yet."

## What every concept must contain

- a specific couple truth
- a visual proof
- an emotional reveal
- a reason someone would send it
- a fresh setup
- identity-safe framing
- low-to-medium anatomy risk
- a line that does not over-explain

## The one-sentence test

Can this be described as: "Love is hidden in the way they ____"?
If not, the concept is probably generic.

## The delete-copy test

If we remove the copy, does the image still communicate at least half the
emotion? If not, the visual plan is weak.

## The screenshot test

Would this feel like a tiny moment someone actually saw, not a content prompt?
If not, repair.

## The cleverness trap

Clever titles are not emotional truth. Reject concepts where the title is better
than the moment.

- Bad: "The Pre-Order"
- Better: He ordered for the no too.
- Best: The second plate is already waiting before she reaches.
