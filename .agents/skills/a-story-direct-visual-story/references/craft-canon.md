## The core model

Direct from intention to visible proof:

```text
relationship truth
  -> physical event
  -> staged action / reaction / consequence
  -> camera + blocking + production design
  -> sequence change
  -> copy-hidden read
  -> rendered-frame audit
```

Style is applied after the event reads.

## Director card

Before drawing or prompting, answer:

- Facts: What is physically true before this scene begins?
- Objective: What does each person want right now?
- Action: What do they visibly do to pursue it?
- Reaction: What changes in the other person, object, or space?
- Point of view: Whose feeling organizes the view?
- Subtext: What is happening beneath the spoken line?
- Change: What is different when the beat ends?
- Silent inference: What should a stranger understand without the copy?

If these cannot be stated concretely, art direction is premature.

## Visual sentence and evidence ledger

A visual sentence has a subject, observable action, target/object, and
reaction/consequence. Evidence can live in:

- a hand starting, stopping, offering, hiding, taking, or returning;
- gaze direction or refusal of gaze;
- feet and torso direction;
- distance, touch, overlap, or occlusion;
- who owns or holds an object;
- an object’s changed position, damage, absence, residue, or readiness;
- wardrobe and environmental traces of a prior event;
- another person’s reaction;
- a before/after difference across frames.

The evidence should support a culturally legible inference. Avoid private
symbols the creator has to explain and stereotype-based shorthand.

## Beat and sequence grammar

Useful narrative jobs, adapted lightly from visual-narrative research:

- Establisher: introduces people, place, or relationship geometry.
- Initial: begins pressure, desire, or action.
- Prolongation: holds or complicates the action.
- Peak: supplies the decisive event or highest information beat.
- Release: shows consequence, reaction, or resolution.

Not every story needs all five, and one frame can combine jobs. The diagnostic
question is whether each swipe/cut supplies new information. Deliberate omission
can invite the viewer to complete an action, but accidental gaps create
confusion.

## Camera as meaning

Choose a camera position after blocking the action.

- Wide: geography, power, isolation, simultaneous actions, or spatial change.
- Medium: relationship behavior and conversation-scale action.
- Close or insert: decisive evidence, touch, object state, or emotional
  punctuation.
- Reaction view: tells the audience how an event lands.
- Over-shoulder or obstructed view: point of view, distance, surveillance, or
  partial understanding.

Shot labels are not variety by themselves. Record why the view serves the
beat, what the focal subject is, and how the next frame changes the visual
information.

## Blocking as dialogue

Stage:

- feet and direction of movement;
- hands and object ownership;
- eye-line and attention target;
- torso orientation and weight;
- body distance, touch, height, and overlap;
- who enters, exits, waits, withdraws, pursues, protects, teases, or yields.

Block equal partnership and actual character. Do not default to one person as
the problem and the other as the handler.

## Production design as biography

For every location ask:

- Who has lived here?
- What just happened?
- What is unfinished?
- What would only this couple leave behind?
- Which practical light source motivates the scene?
- Which one or two details alter the story rather than fill space?

An object constellation should have causal relationships, not be a list of
cozy-home nouns. A prepared cup, snapped key, relocated charger, damp towel, or
pair of shoes can reveal unseen action and history. Plants, books, chai, and
fairy lights do not become story evidence merely by appearing together.

## Visual arc

Bruce Block’s visual components—space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, and
rhythm—are useful variables. Select one or two to track the emotional arc.
Examples:

- body distance contracts while irritation remains;
- one object changes hands and therefore meaning;
- stable vertical space gains diagonals under pressure, then settles;
- motion moves from crossed paths to synchronized action;
- repeated framing returns with one consequential detail changed.

Continuity is authored repetition; variety is authored change. Both need a
story reason.

## Word-image division of labour

Useful relationships:

- Additive: image supplies behavior/evidence; words add interiority or precision.
- Counterpoint: words and image create an intentional, legible tension.
- Interdependent: neither carries the full meaning alone.

Redundant pairing repeats the same information without adding meaning. The
deletion test is diagnostic: hide the words and report what story survives;
then hide the image and ask what unique work it was doing.

## Storyboard loop

Rough boards are thinking tools, not drawing competitions:

1. break the event into minor beats;
2. stage and frame only the necessary moments;
3. after copy lock, expose only observable cards to a fresh reader—no copy,
   intent labels, expected inference, or story reasons;
4. record what they infer before explaining;
5. reboard the weak layer;
6. time and sequence it before expensive generation.

The proof slide should be the highest semantic risk, not automatically the
cover. If a proof fails, first decide whether the idea, evidence, staging,
sequence, or generation drift caused the failure.

## Short-form translation

Story comes first; platform translation follows.

- Establish the visible situation or question immediately.
- Compose each required native aspect ratio independently.
- Protect essential evidence and text from interface-safe zones.
- Use motion and sound because the beat calls for them, not to imitate pace.
- Review retention as a clue, not as proof of comprehension or delight.

Fast cutting cannot rescue frames that do not change the story.
